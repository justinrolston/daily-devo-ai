June 26, 2026

You shall not take vengeance or bear a grudge against the sons of your own people, but you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD.
Leviticus 19:18 (ESV)

When Love Refuses to Keep Score

Reflection:

What does this text teach us? Nestled within a chapter of practical holiness laws, God gives Israel a command that cuts straight to the heart: do not take vengeance, do not hold a grudge, but love your neighbor as yourself. Notice the progression—God moves from external behavior (vengeance) to internal posture (grudge-bearing) to positive action (love). It’s not enough to simply refrain from retaliation; the heart itself must be transformed toward genuine care for others.

The phrase “sons of your own people” reminds us this command was first given within the covenant community, but Jesus expands its scope dramatically. When asked “who is my neighbor?” He answered with the parable of the Good Samaritan, shattering any notion that love could be confined to those like us (Luke 10:25-37). Christ himself is the ultimate fulfillment of this law—loving His enemies, praying for those who crucified Him, laying down His life for people who had wronged God completely.

Here’s the sobering truth: none of us naturally love this way. Our default is self-protection, score-keeping, and nursing wounds. We convince ourselves that holding a grudge is justice, when Scripture calls it what it is—sin that corrodes the soul and denies the sufficiency of God’s justice. The command ends with “I am the LORD”—a reminder that vengeance belongs to Him alone (Romans 12:19), and that our capacity to forgive flows from trusting His perfect justice rather than administering our own.

This is gospel work, not willpower work. We love because He first loved us (1 John 4:19), extending grace to us while we were still His enemies. The Spirit who indwells believers is actively conforming us into people who release grudges, seek reconciliation, and treat others’ wellbeing as our own—not perfectly, but progressively, as evidence of new life in Christ.

Confessional Tie-in:

“What does the ninth commandment require? …the preserving and promoting of truth between man and man, and of our own and our neighbor’s good name…” — Westminster Larger Catechism, Q. 144

Prayer Prompt:

Lord, I confess the grudges I’ve held longer than I’ve admitted, even to myself. Thank You for loving me while I was Your enemy, and for the cross where true justice and mercy meet. Give me grace today to release what I’ve been clutching, and to love someone who has wronged me as You have loved me.

Go Deeper:

Is there a specific person toward whom you’re harboring resentment right now? Consider writing their name down and praying specifically for their good this week—not because they’ve earned it, but because Christ has called and equipped you to love this way. Read Romans 12:14-21 for further meditation on how gospel-shaped love responds to wrong.
