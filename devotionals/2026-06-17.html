Sing to God, sing praises to his name; lift up a song to him who rides through the deserts; his name is the LORD; exult before him! Father of the fatherless and protector of widows is God in his holy habitation.
Psalm 68:4-5 (ESV)

The God Who Rides Through Deserts

Reflection:

What does this text teach us? The psalmist calls Israel to worship with exuberant praise—singing, lifting up songs, exulting before the LORD. But notice the picture given of God: he “rides through the deserts.” This image recalls God’s leading Israel through the wilderness, present with his people in the most desolate, barren places. He is not a distant deity confined to comfortable temples; he is the God who moves through the wasteland with his people.

Then the psalm turns to something remarkable. This majestic, desert-riding God who commands the heavens is the same God who is “Father of the fatherless and protector of widows.” The transition is striking. The one enthroned in glory stoops to care for the most vulnerable, the most overlooked, the most humanly hopeless among us. Orphans and widows in the ancient world had no social safety net, no legal standing, no protector—unless God himself took up their cause. And he does.

This is who God has always been. Throughout Scripture, he identifies himself with the weak, the marginalized, the broken. This reaches its fullest expression in Christ, who entered our wilderness, bore our griefs, and was himself forsaken on the cross so that we—spiritually orphaned by sin—could be adopted as children of God (Ephesians 1:5). The desert-riding King became the wilderness-wandering Savior for us.

If you feel fatherless today—abandoned, unseen, without an advocate—this text speaks directly to you. God’s holiness is not aloofness; his greatness is not indifference. He rules the cosmos and tends to the brokenhearted in the same breath. Your desert season, whatever form it takes, is not outside his reach.

This truth also compels us outward. If God defines himself as protector of the vulnerable, his people—being conformed to his image—must reflect this same heart toward orphans, widows, and the marginalized around us. Worship that ignores mercy is incomplete worship.

Confessional Tie-in:

“God, having made all things, doth uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures, actions, and things, from the greatest even to the least, by his most wise and holy providence… in his fatherly care.” — Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter V (on Providence)

Prayer Prompt:

Lord, you are both exalted above the heavens and near to the brokenhearted—I praise you for this holy paradox. Forgive me for the times I’ve doubted your care in my wilderness or ignored the vulnerable around me. Be my Father in the desert places, and make me an instrument of your protection to those who have none.

Go Deeper:

This week, identify one practical way you can reflect God’s heart toward the vulnerable—a widow in your congregation, a struggling single parent, a child in foster care. Consider journaling about a “desert” season in your own life and where you can now see God’s presence in retrospect. Read Deuteronomy 10:17-19 to see how God ties his own character to his command that we love the sojourner and vulnerable.
