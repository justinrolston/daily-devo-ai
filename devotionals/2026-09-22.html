September 22, 2026

Therefore welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God.
Romans 15:7 (ESV)

Welcomed to Welcome

Reflection:

What does this text teach us? Paul writes to a church wrestling with real division—Jewish and Gentile believers disagreed sharply over dietary laws, sacred days, and cultural practices. Rather than telling one side to simply win the argument, Paul points them to something deeper: the pattern of Christ’s own welcome. “Welcome one another,” he writes, “as Christ has welcomed you.” The command flows directly from the gospel itself.

Notice the logic. We are not told to welcome others based on their lovability, their agreement with us, or their spiritual maturity. We are told to welcome them the way Christ welcomed us—while we were still sinners, still confused, still prone to wander. His welcome wasn’t earned; it was extended. It cost him everything and gained us everything. If that’s the model, then our welcome of one another can’t be conditional on comfort or compatibility.

This matters because the church has always struggled here. We gravitate toward people who look like us, think like us, and vote like us. We build subtle hierarchies of acceptance. But Paul insists that the same grace that reached across the infinite gap between holy God and sinful humanity must now reach across the much smaller gaps between believers—differences of background, personality, conviction, and preference.

Notice too the purpose clause: “for the glory of God.” Our welcome of one another isn’t merely social kindness; it’s worship. When a fractured, diverse body of believers is bound together in genuine love, it displays something the watching world cannot manufacture—a unity rooted not in shared taste but in shared grace. This is sanctification lived out in community: the Spirit slowly dismantling our instinct toward self-protective exclusion and replacing it with Christ’s own posture of open-armed welcome.

This will cost you something. It may mean sitting with someone whose politics unsettle you, extending patience to a believer who processes faith differently, or setting aside your right to be right in order to preserve the bond of peace. But remember—you were once the outsider Christ welcomed in. Let that memory soften your hands and open your door.

Confessional Tie-in:

“The communion which the saints have with Christ, doth not make them in any wise partakers of the substance of his Godhead… nor take away or diminish their propriety in, or ownership of, their goods and possessions. Saints by profession are bound to maintain a holy fellowship and communion in the worship of God, and in performing such other spiritual services as tend to their mutual edification.” — Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter 26

Prayer Prompt:

Lord, thank you for welcoming me in Christ when I had nothing to offer you but my sin. Forgive me for the ways I withhold welcome from others—guarding my comfort, nursing my preferences, keeping my circle small. Give me a heart that reflects your grace, and show me one person today I can welcome more fully.

Go Deeper:

Think of one believer you find difficult to welcome—perhaps due to personality, background, or disagreement. This week, take one concrete step toward them: a conversation, an invitation, a prayer for them by name. For further meditation, read Ephesians 2:11–22 and consider how Christ’s cross doesn’t just save individuals but creates a new, welcoming community.
